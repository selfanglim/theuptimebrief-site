The Credit Behind the Compute
October 2026
Data Centres · Credit Structuring · APAC
The Credit Behind the Compute — two Hut 8 project bonds, two different credit chains, the same investment-grade outcome

In 2026, Hut 8 raised US$7.5 billion across two data-centre project-bond financings — River Bend, leased to neocloud Fluidstack with a Google backstop on specified obligations, and Beacon Point, leased directly to an unnamed high-investment-grade tenant with no separate backstop disclosed. Both reached the investment-grade, non-recourse bond market on coupons 6.3 basis points apart, despite different ratings (BBB− vs Baa2 — a cross-agency equivalence, not a same-agency comparison) and payment-support architectures. The comparison doesn't show the market has stopped caring about tenant credit. It shows tenant credit is one input into a transaction-specific assessment of payment, protection, timing, resilience and recovery — not a single screening test. If a data-centre operator depends on a handful of large customers, how much of that contracted growth becomes resilient cash flow for the operator's own investors?

Who Captures the Control Point?
October 2026
Data Centres · Infrastructure Finance · APAC
Who Captures the Control Point? — The Financeable MW — what converts physical scarcity into underwritable cash flow

A megawatt can be Nominal, Controlled, Deliverable, Revenue — or Financeable. The fifth state is not the next step in the MW-State Framework: it is a separate gate, imposed by capital markets, not engineering. Passing it requires five bankability conditions: counterparty credit quality, contract durability, physical delivery credibility, collateral quality, and risk allocation. Hut 8's US$4.25bn non-recourse bond on 352 MW of critical IT capacity and Meta's ~US$27bn JV development exposure define what the gate looks like when passed at scale. The US$88bn AI leveraged-finance signal tells you capital is ready. The bankability gates tell you which MW gets to use it.

100 MW ≠ 100 MW — why the state of a megawatt matters as much as the size of the pipeline

A data-centre developer can announce 100 MW. Another can announce the same 100 MW. On a pipeline chart, they look identical. From an investor's perspective, they may be very different assets. Where Inference Has to Run — inference doesn't migrate to one new geography, it splits location into workload-specific placement envelopes

OpenAI and Mistral now both charge a 10% uplift for eligible geographically constrained processing — the market pricing "here, not there" explicitly. But that surcharge accrues at the model-service layer, not to the facility hosting the endpoint, and inference is pulling in two directions at once: cloud pooling can make regions interchangeable for workloads that don't need to stay local, while latency, sovereignty and enterprise data gravity pin others to a much narrower set of locations. This edition builds a placement-envelope framework for underwriting which workloads can be pooled and which can't — and argues that in the inference era, the most valuable megawatt may not be the closest one. The Returns Inversion
August 2026
Data Centres · Capital Allocation · APAC
The Returns Inversion — why the cheapest megawatt to build may not be the most valuable megawatt to own

Across APAC the cost gap looks decisive: Cushman & Wakefield puts a mid-spec Singapore build near US$14.4m/MW against roughly US$9.6m in Malaysia, and Wood Mackenzie's hyperscale power bill runs ~US$123m a year in Singapore versus ~US$42m in Indonesia. So why doesn't the owner of the cheaper asset simply keep the difference — and why did Singapore just provisionally allocate another 200 MW on Jurong Island, its most constrained, highest-baseline market? Because a cost advantage at a location is not a cost advantage in an owner's cash flow. It has to survive the lease structure, competitive pass-through, customer eligibility, utilisation and the business model before any of it reaches equity. This edition proves non-equivalence — a location's cost advantage and an owner's return advantage are different things — and gives a seven-question test for which megawatt you are actually buying, and who keeps the economics.

Whose Credit Sits Behind the Rent? — when a household name stands behind AI data-centre rent, "backed" is not one structure

In 2026 several of the largest AI infrastructure financings put a stronger balance sheet behind an unrated tenant's rent — Google supports specified Fluidstack lease obligations; Broadcom disclosed ~US$29bn of maximum exposure inside a US$35bn AI-rack platform; Nvidia disclosed up to US$105bn tied to OpenAI's twenty-year Ohio lease. But they don't do the same thing: the support sits in different places, protects different obligations, and starts under different conditions — and the name that gives investors comfort may not even be the party owing the rent. A guarantee doesn't remove risk; it reallocates it. This edition is a field guide to reading what the guarantee actually covers, when it starts, and when it ends — because "backed" is not a risk grade.

Scarcity Doesn't Equal Return — power-secured capacity is scarce in Greater Seoul, but a scarcity premium only becomes a return if it survives to investor IRR

Greater Seoul's grid is a genuine bottleneck: of 522 power-system applications to March 2026, only 10 cleared final approval, and rents have run up over 70% since 2019. But a scarcity premium isn't a return — it has to survive three leaks before it reaches investor IRR. The lease can hold contractual rent below market inside a fund's holding period; fully-fitted structures can leave reinvestment CapEx with the landlord as GPU density climbs; and an asset built for today's tenant may not clear tomorrow's buyer. Read through Keppel's greenfield Ansan entry, Korea is the evidence — the subject is the principle: scarcity creates optionality, structure decides how much becomes IRR.

REITs · Capital Markets · APAC
Premiums Don't Raise Capital. Sponsors Do. — two data centre REITs trade above NAV, but convert those premiums into capital in very different ways

Keppel DC REIT trades at a sustained ~1.32x its reported NAV; the GDS C-REIT (Southern GDS Data Centre REIT) has swung across roughly 1.28x–1.49x over three official closes, including a spike that triggered an exchange trading suspension. Both look like scarcity premiums — but a premium-to-NAV is not a financing mechanism. What creates value is how a sponsor converts it into acquisition capital: Keppel DC REIT through a conventional preferential offering into a deep secondary market, the GDS C-REIT through a rule-priced directed placement (定向扩募). Same-looking premium, different market structure, different playbook — and the difference decides who gets paid when the next asset is funded.
