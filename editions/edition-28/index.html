The Cost of Confidence

When equity-market investability meets infrastructure bankability.

By Sel Fang, Lim · Data centre and infrastructure finance, APAC

A data centre does not lose a megawatt because an index provider loses confidence. Its tenants and power contracts do not automatically disappear. Yet the price of capital can change before an asset's operating fundamentals do.

Edition 27, The Concentration Behind the Contract, closed by asking which commitments underpinning a data-centre development become durable cash for investors — and what price they should pay for the uncertainty that remains. This edition shifts the question to the market surrounding those contracts: what happens to an infrastructure investment when international investors begin to question the integrity of the country's equity market itself?

Power, permits and water may remain binding constraints regardless of where the equity index stands. Indonesia is a useful test of both propositions. The real issue is not whether it will still need data centres. It is whether market-level uncertainty reaches project-level financing, and whether the returns on the next delivered megawatt compensate investors for the risks of getting it operational.

1 The deal

On 15 October 2026, MSCI opened a formal consultation on moving Indonesia directly from Emerging Market to Standalone status, citing opaque shareholding structures, suspected coordinated trading and insufficient evidence of sustained enforcement. MSCI said a Frontier classification would not adequately reflect the depth of its concerns. This is not an implemented downgrade. Responses are due 30 April 2027; the final decision is due on or before 30 June 2027. No effective deletion date has been confirmed. [1] [2]

The initial market response was severe. But what followed complicates a simple flight-from-Indonesia story: the Jakarta Composite Index rose 4.90% in the week of 19–23 October, while foreign investors sold a reported Rp6.67 trillion net of shares. By 27 October, the IHSG stood at 5,266.13 and USD/IDR near 18,153 on the Indonesian government's market dashboard. These observations cover different instruments and dates; they are not a single causal measurement. [3] [4]

Foreign selling and a rising local index can coexist. Domestic demand, valuation resets and investor rotation matter. So does the difference between a proposal, an index deletion and the outflows that might eventually accompany it.

A useful counterpoint came from FTSE Russell, which retained Indonesia's Secondary Emerging Market classification in results published on 6 October from its September annual country-classification review. FTSE and MSCI apply distinct methodologies; neither outcome determines the other's. [6]

For funds committed to tracking affected MSCI Emerging Markets benchmarks, an eventual exclusion could require selling or reweighting. But a consultation is not a sale order, and the mechanism does not compel every active fund, infrastructure lender or hyperscaler to withdraw. [1]

Nor would an Indonesian deletion automatically send capital to Malaysia or Singapore data-centre assets. Passive index rebalancing, active portfolio allocation and greenfield infrastructure FDI (foreign direct investment) are three different capital decisions. An investor selling Indonesian listed shares can buy securities anywhere permitted by its benchmark; a cloud provider choosing a new campus is deciding on demand, latency, data regulation, power, land, permits and long-term returns.

Argentina provides a limited precedent: MSCI moved it directly from Emerging to Standalone in 2021, but that decision centred on capital controls. Indonesia's consultation centres on market transparency and trading integrity. The index mechanism is comparable; the macroeconomic consequences should not be assumed to be. [2]

The first investment test is therefore market investability: can institutions gain transparent, liquid exposure to listed equities? The second is project bankability: can lenders rely on enforceable cash flows, sponsor strength, currency matching, permits, delivered utilities and adequate downside protection? One cannot substitute for the other.

2 The engineering read

Consider two Indonesian financing examples announced before the MSCI consultation. DCI Indonesia signed a Rp17 trillion investment-credit agreement with BCA on 30 April 2026, publicly reported on 7 May. BDx announced a US$320 million-equivalent facility from a group of Indonesian banks in April. The DCI amount is expressed in rupiah; BDx's headline amount is in US dollars, but that does not establish the contractual debt currency. BDx has described matching financing obligations with rupiah revenue and expenditure as a risk-management consideration. Neither announcement provides evidence of financing margins changing after MSCI. [10] [11]

This is why debt denomination, not a press-release headline, drives the FX analysis. An operator with IDR revenue and unhedged USD debt must find more rupiah for each fixed dollar of debt service when USD/IDR rises. An operator with IDR revenue and IDR debt has a better currency match, although domestic interest rates and imported equipment costs still matter. USD-linked revenue, hedging and contractual pass-through can change the exposure again.

Take a deliberately hypothetical US$100 million project financed with US$60 million debt and US$40 million equity, generating US$13 million equivalent of annual cash available for debt service. Under the frozen E28 model:

But financing is not the only gate. West Java authorities ordered a temporary stop at BDx's Jatiluhur development over outstanding environmental and building approvals at the beginning of October. In Batam, BP Batam has directed data-centre developers toward independent seawater reverse-osmosis (SWRO) facilities for full operations, with implementation details still project-specific. Neither development was caused by MSCI; nor should household water shortages be attributed to data-centre consumption without evidence. [12] [13]

For an infrastructure investor, the underwriting calculation is therefore not just what capital costs, but how long it takes to start earning cash flow.

On 22 October, Danantara officials discussed a Bring Your Own Energy (BYOE) approach for very large data-centre projects — for example, above 500 MW — and potential electricity offtake commitments around 10–15 years. This was a reported investment expectation, not a verified nationwide statutory threshold or signed power purchase agreement (PPA). It does not imply complete independence from PLN or guaranteed uptime. [14]

The investment-committee question is who funds the generation and interconnection, who bears delivery risk, and when contracted data-centre revenue can actually begin. A grid connection may require less sponsor equity if capacity exists. A separately financed power producer and long-term PPA may isolate generation capex, but create payment guarantees and completion interfaces. Integrated data-centre-plus-generation ownership may offer more control, but introduces another capital-intensive construction project.

3 The capital allocation read

Some pressure has spread beyond the equity market. Bank Indonesia acknowledged outflows from government securities (SBN) and its rupiah securities (SRBI) around the MSCI warning, while describing the shock as manageable. A single-provider indicative series showed Indonesia's five-year sovereign CDS moving from 93.28 basis points on 15 October to 106.47 on 23 October — a rise of 13.19 bp. The government dashboard put the ten-year domestic sovereign yield near 7.50% on 27 October; ADB AsianBondsOnline separately reported 7.468% on 26 October. These are two different data series and dated observations, not a matched yield-change calculation. These observations are evidence of market repricing following the announcement, not proof that MSCI alone caused the movements. [8] [9] [4] [5]

Bank Indonesia's 21 October decision also resists a simplistic reading. It held the headline BI-Rate at 5.75% and the Lending Facility rate at 6.50%, while raising the Deposit Facility rate to 5.00% and expanding incentives for domestic non-deliverable forwards (DNDF). The central bank cited external uncertainty and currency stability; it did not announce an MSCI-triggered headline rate hike. [7]

E1 vs E2: the critical distinction

E1 — Market credit: foreign portfolio withdrawals, FX pressure and movement in sovereign-risk indicators are observable. Their attribution to MSCI versus global yields, the dollar and existing domestic pressures is still incomplete. E2 — Funding credit: a change in data-centre loan margins, leverage limits, tenor, covenants or drawdown conditions has not been demonstrated by a comparable post-15-October transaction in the reviewed evidence. A widening sovereign CDS quotation is not a revised project-finance term sheet.

The regional comparison is not one-sided. Mirae Asset's September model estimated a 15.1% project IRR for a hypothetical 100MW Batam hyperscale development versus 13.4% for Johor at the same modelled lease rate — a 170-basis-point difference using the displayed rounded figures. Yet it also estimated 36 months to energise a 100MW project in Indonesia versus 12 months in Malaysia, while reporting a Jakarta colocation vacancy rate of 20.4% and Johor's at 0.7%. These are analyst assumptions and market snapshots, not realised project returns or universal energisation schedules. [15]

That tension matters more than calling a predetermined regional winner. A higher modelled IRR can be consumed by delayed revenue, water infrastructure, extra power capex or financing uncertainty. Conversely, a financeable power arrangement that accelerates energisation could justify additional upfront investment.

Domestic demand and regulation also anchor investment. GR71/2019 allows private electronic-system operators to process or store data abroad subject to oversight and access conditions, while imposing different requirements on public-sector systems. Banking-specific rules under POJK 11/2022 generally require domestic placement of certain systems unless approval is obtained. This is not a blanket Indonesia-only rule for every cloud customer. [16] [17]

Google, Microsoft and AWS have announced substantial Indonesian cloud infrastructure activity, but announced commitments differ in scope and include operating expenditure, software, AI initiatives and training in some cases. No verified operator announcement in this research establishes that MSCI's proposal has redirected the next hyperscale campus away from Indonesia. Existing commitments may be resilient; discretionary future phases are a different decision.

4 What this means for the broader market

Indonesia is pursuing reforms, not simply awaiting MSCI's decision. OJK (Otoritas Jasa Keuangan) reiterated its shareholder-disclosure and enforcement agenda on 16 October. [18]

Enforcement was not entirely prospective. On 16 October, OJK imposed approximately Rp2.1 trillion in administrative fines on parties involved in DSSA share trading and the BREN and CUAN IPOs. The underlying investigations began before MSCI's 15 October consultation. These sanctions demonstrate actual enforcement activity, but whether they lead to sustained improvements in ownership transparency and market integrity sufficient to satisfy MSCI remains untested. [19] [20]

Separately, IDX (Indonesia Stock Exchange) announced high-shareholding-concentration index exclusions on 22 October for implementation from 2 November. On 26 October, IDX also announced plans to add independent participants to its high-shareholding-concentration committee and strengthen investigations into suspected coordinated trading. On 28 October, IDX leadership said Danantara had proposed taking a stake in the exchange as part of demutualisation. A proposal is not a completed purchase, and an ownership change is not itself proof of stronger trading integrity. OJK's POJK 13/2026, effective 17 September, generally limits a shareholder to 5% of IDX shares; larger holdings require OJK approval, while majority ownership is prohibited outright, including through affiliates. [21] [22] [23]

My forward tests are simple: does actual enforcement change transparency and market behaviour? Do foreign outflows, FX pressure and sovereign spreads stabilise relative to global drivers? Do new project lenders demand different pricing or covenants? Does a real BYOE project disclose who carries the generation, PPA and energisation risks? And do new hyperscaler site choices demonstrate a changed geographical allocation — rather than commentators merely predicting one?

There are at least two plausible outcomes. Reform could improve market access while well-structured infrastructure projects continue raising capital. Or confidence could stay weak long enough to increase the required return for new, uncommitted projects. Neither outcome is proven today.

Market confidence cannot generate megawatts, secure a grid connection or sign a lease. But it can change the price investors demand for turning those physical assets into reliable cash flows. Equally, low-cost capital cannot rescue a project whose permits, water or power fail to arrive on time.

The question for the next investment committee is not simply where Southeast Asia's next data centre will be announced. It is where the next megawatt can be delivered, financed and operated at a return that compensates for every risk along the way.

When contracted demand and financing are in place but energisation or commissioning slips, who actually bears the delay — the developer, power supplier, contractor, lender or tenant?

That is the question I will carry into Edition 29.

Verification notes
[PRIMARY] — The IHSG's 4.90% weekly gain and Rp6.67 trillion in foreign net selling relate to 19–23 October 2026, confirmed from IDX weekly statistics — not a single-session rebound. The government dashboard's IHSG and USD/IDR observations are separately dated 27 October 2026. These are separate dated observations, not one causal measurement.
[INDICATIVE] — The +13.19bp CDS widening figure is drawn from a single-provider indicative historical series, not a multi-dealer composite such as Bloomberg. It is reported at this confidence tier accordingly.
[MODELLED] — The DSCR scenarios (base 1.52×/10.4%; combined stress 1.26×/5.2%) reflect a US$100m project financed with US$60m debt and US$40m equity, amortising over ten annual repayments. They are illustrative outputs of a frozen model and are not drawn from any specific project, transaction or lender term sheet.
[UNCONFIRMED / E2] — No project-loan repricing (E2) for Indonesian data-centre infrastructure has been confirmed in the public record as of the research cut-off (28 October 2026, 14:44 SGT). Any reference to E2 in this edition is framed as a potential transmission, not a confirmed event.
[CONSULTATION] — All references to the MSCI process use the terms “consultation,” “proposed,” or “proposal” in line with MSCI's own published language. No index decision has been confirmed. The MSCI consultation (15 October) precedes, and cannot be the cause of, any market movement dated before that day.
[HOUSE VIEW] — The E1/E2 analytical framework and the Portfolio Framework / Investment Lens are the author's analytical framing, not conclusions drawn from any specific disclosed transaction.
Research discipline. This edition maintains a strict distinction between observed market events (E1) and potential financing-market consequences (E2). The DSCR model is illustrative and frozen; its outputs should not be applied to any specific project without independent modelling of that project's actual loan terms, revenue structure and covenant package. The MSCI consultation status is described as pending as of the research cut-off.
